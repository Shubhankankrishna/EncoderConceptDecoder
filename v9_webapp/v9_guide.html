The V9 experiments, from zero

Six experiments, S1 to S6, that all ask one question: can a small language model think in whole steps instead of single words, and can we search over those steps? This page assumes you have never trained a neural network.

  • S2 running
  • S3 to S6 designed, not run yet

Start here

The big idea in sixty seconds

A language model writes text one small piece at a time. V9 asks what happens if it also keeps a short summary note for every four pieces and learns to guess the next note before it writes the words.

Here is the whole project in plain words. A normal language model reads a piece of text and guesses the next small piece, called a token. It does this over and over, and that is how it writes.

V9 adds one more habit. After every four tokens the model writes a short summary note and keeps it. We call that note a concept. The model is also trained to guess the next concept before it has written the words that belong to it. That is a bit like glancing at the next chapter title before you read the chapter.

If this works, two useful things become possible:

  • Cheap what-if thinking. Looking at a summary note costs far less than writing out the words. A model could try several different next ideas and only write words for the best one.
  • Planning ahead. With cheap what-ifs, the model can plan a few steps ahead the way a chess engine does, and then write.

Whether any of this really works is the open question. The six experiments below are the way we find out, one claim at a time.

Why six experiments and not one big one?

The earlier versions of this project (V1 to V8) kept changing many things at once. When a result came out good or bad, nobody could say which change caused it. V9 follows a simple rule: each experiment changes one thing and measures it against a control. A control is the same setup with that one thing switched off.

Idea

Think of a taste test. To learn whether adding salt helps a soup, you cook two pots that differ only in salt. If you also change the onions, the stock and the pot, you learn nothing about salt.

Every experiment also writes down, before it runs, what would count as success. This is called pre-registration. The pass or fail rules are called gates, and you will meet them in each chapter.

Chapter 1 · the basics

Five ideas you need first

Tokens, guessing, surprise, walking downhill, and blocks. If you know these, every later chapter is readable. If you already know them, skip to the next chapter.

1. A computer only understands numbers

A computer cannot read the letter "c". It can only do arithmetic. So the first step of any language model is to turn text into numbers.

The usual way is to cut the text into pieces called tokens and give each piece an ID number from a fixed list called the vocabulary. Our models use a vocabulary of 2,048 pieces. Common words get a piece of their own. Rare words are cut into smaller pieces.

After this step the model never sees letters again. Each ID is swapped for a list of 128 numbers called a vector. Picture a vector as the coordinates of a point on a map with 128 directions. Pieces that behave alike end up close together. The model's whole job is to move and mix these points until the last one lands near the right next piece.

2. A language model is a guessing machine

A language model has one job. Given the tokens so far, it gives every token in the vocabulary a probability of coming next. The probabilities add up to 1. To write, it picks one token from that list, adds it to the text, and repeats.

Pause and think

Look at the "fork" situation: left and right are almost equally likely, and both are fine. If the model always picks its favourite, it hides the other good answer. Remember this picture. It comes back in the S2 chapter as the main reason S2 exists.

3. Learning means being less surprised

To train a model we need a score for how wrong it is. The standard score is its surprise at the real answer. Take the probability the model gave to the token that actually came next, and compute minus its natural logarithm. That number is the loss.

If the model was sure and right (probability 1), the loss is 0. If it gave the true token only 1 chance in 100, the loss is 4.6. A model that knows nothing guesses evenly over 2,048 tokens, which gives a loss of 7.6. When you read about a text loss falling from about 7.7 to about 2.9, that is a model going from clueless to usefully surprised-less.

4. How the guessing improves: walking downhill

The model is a large pile of numbers called parameters (or weights). Our main model has about 2.4 million. Training adjusts them so that the loss goes down.

For each parameter, calculus tells us whether raising it would raise or lower the loss. That slope is the gradient. We nudge every parameter a small step in the direction that lowers the loss, then repeat millions of times. This is gradient descent.

Analogy

You are on a foggy hillside and want the valley. You cannot see it, but you can feel the slope under your feet, so you step downhill. The size of your step is the learning rate.

Idea

Where you start changes where you end up. A training run starts from random numbers, and the dice roll that sets them is called the seed. The same recipe with a different seed can behave very differently. You will see a striking real example in S1, and it is the reason every comparison in this project uses at least two seeds.

5. Inside the guesser: attention and blocks

Between the vectors that go in and the probabilities that come out sits a stack of layers called blocks. Each block does two things.

  1. Attention: look back.

    Every position looks at the earlier positions and decides which ones matter for it. It collects a weighted blend of what it finds. It is only allowed to look backwards, because looking ahead would be cheating on a guessing task. This is called causal attention.

  2. Feed-forward: think.

    Each position then thinks on its own about what it gathered, using a small network.

Both steps add their result to the vector instead of replacing it. This means nothing is lost along the way. A model with ten blocks does "look, then think" ten times.

Small on purpose

Chat assistants have dozens of blocks and vectors with thousands of numbers. Ours has 10 blocks and vectors of 128. It trains in hours on a laptop, which lets us run many careful experiments. The price is that some things that work at large scale may not show up here. The project says so openly wherever it matters.

Chapter 2 · the exam every model takes

GraphNav: a tiny world of one-way roads

All six experiments use the same exam. Learn it once here and you can follow every later chapter.

A tiny world of one-way roads

The exam is called GraphNav, short for graph navigation. Picture ten towns. From every town exactly three one-way roads leave: one blue, one orange and one green. Each road leads to some other town. You get a start town and a goal town, and you must write down which roads to take to get there.

This looks like a children's game, and that is deliberate. A small ordinary program can check an answer exactly, new puzzles can be made forever, and every step of an answer is a clean, separate decision. Play one below.

How the puzzle is written for the model

The model cannot see a picture. It gets a flat list of tokens, laid out like this.

  1. The instruction.

    Four tokens: a marker meaning "navigate", the goal town, the start town, and a separator.

  2. The map.

    All 30 roads, in shuffled order. Each road is four tokens: from-town, colour, to-town, separator.

  3. The answer, written by the model.

    One group of four tokens per hop: colour, a filler word meaning "to", the town, and a separator. After the last hop the model writes four more tokens that mean DONE.

The filler tokens exist for one reason: they make every group exactly four tokens long. That regularity is what the rest of the project builds on.

The verifier

A small program, not a model, checks the answer. Every hop must be a real road out of the town you are in. DONE is only legal when you stand on the goal after at least one hop. If anything is off, the answer fails, and the program says why (illegal move, wrong goal, missing DONE, and so on).

Four tokens, one step

Every road in the puzzle and every hop in the answer is exactly four tokens. So if you cut the text into groups of four, each group is exactly one road or exactly one hop.

This is the most important fact in the guide. It means a group of four tokens, which we call a chunk, is a natural unit of meaning on this exam: one chunk is one reasoning step. Later we will summarise each chunk into a single vector called a concept, and a concept will then stand for one step of a plan.

Where this stops being true

In ordinary English, four tokens are not a thought. The project claims things only for this exam. Searching over steps in real text is listed as future work, because nobody has a clean way yet to cut text into steps.

Teaching a tiny model to read in chunks

If a model keeps one summary note per four tokens and is trained to guess the next note, does it solve the exam better than a plain model of the same size?

In one breath

  • We build three small models from scratch: a plain one, a chunk-reader that also guesses the next concept, and the same chunk-reader without the guessing.
  • Each is trained twice from different random starts (seeds 901 and 902), for 24,000 updates.
  • Finished on 7 October 2026. The models turned out to be much less steady than hoped, and the guessing task did not reliably help.

Step 0: the plain model, our control

Before building anything new, we need something to compare against. The control is an ordinary model of the kind that powers chat assistants: ten blocks stacked on top of each other, every block looking back over all earlier tokens. We call it A_flat. It has 2,450,048 parameters.

"Flat" means it has no idea that groups of four tokens matter. To it, a road in the puzzle is just four unrelated tokens in a row.

Step 1: the same ten blocks, rearranged into three jobs

The new model has the same number of blocks, ten, but splits them into three jobs. The split is the whole idea. Step through it below.

  1. Encoder, 3 blocks.

    Reads the real tokens and gives each one a vector that knows its context. This is ordinary language-model work.

  2. Pooling.

    Averages the four vectors of each chunk into one, then rescales it. The result is a concept: a summary note for the chunk.

  3. Concept stack, 4 blocks.

    Reads only the concepts, never the tokens. With four times fewer positions, each block is cheap. Reasoning here is reasoning about steps.

  4. Guess head.

    Turns the concept stack's output into a guess of the next concept. More in the next step.

  5. Decoder, 3 blocks.

    Writes the answer tokens. It still reads the real tokens, and also receives the guess as a hint, one chunk late.

Step 2: guessing the next concept (NCP)

NCP stands for next-concept prediction. A language model guesses the next token. Here we also ask the model to guess the next concept, which is the summary note of the chunk it has not written yet. We know the true answer, because once the chunk exists we can pool it. The loss is how far the guess is from the truth.

How does a model produce a vector as a guess? It does not invent numbers freely. It owns a fixed set of stickers: a codebook of reusable vectors. For each slice of the concept, the model gives every sticker a weight, and the guess is the weighted average of the stickers. Our concept has 128 numbers, cut into 8 slices of 16. Each slice has its own 32 stickers.

Jargon

The sticker idea is called product quantization, and "soft" means the weights are probabilities, not a single hard pick. The codebook is trained with a small extra loss, the VQ loss, that keeps the stickers close to real concepts and keeps all of them in use.

Step 3: how the guess helps the writer

The decoder's input for each token is the token's own vector plus the hint. The hint from chunk j is added at the last token of that chunk, so it arrives just in time to steer the first token of chunk j + 1. There is also a second, smaller channel called the port, a plain vector sent through the same gap and scaled by a learned knob that starts at 0.1.

Show the equations
H_t   = Encoder(embed(x))_t                 token vectors, 128 numbers each
C_j   = RMS( mean( H_4j ... H_4j+3 ) )       concept of chunk j
U_j   = ConceptStack(C_0 ... C_j)_j          only looks at concepts up to j
pi_j,s = softmax( W_s U_j + b_s )            s = 1..8 slices, 32 stickers each
c^_j  = concat_s  sum_n pi_j,s,n * Q_s,n     the guess (soft product quantization)
A_j   = W_A RMS(U_j)                         the port
D_t   = H_t + shift(c^)_t + gamma * shift(A)_t     decoder input
L     = L_token + alpha(u) * L_NCP + L_VQ
L_NCP = mean || c^_j - stopgrad(C_j+1) ||^2   squared distance to the true next concept

The stopgrad means the target is treated as a fixed number, so the model cannot cheat by moving the target toward its guess.

Step 4: do not turn the guessing on right away

An earlier version (V6) switched the guessing task on at full strength from the very first update. On a small set of development puzzles that model scored 0%, while the same model without the task scored 79.7%. V8 then tried a delay: the guessing task counts for nothing for the first 6,000 updates, ramps up to a small weight of 0.1 by update 8,000, and stays there. That is the delayed schedule, and it is the one our main arm uses. The weight is called α (alpha).

An idea, not a fact

Why would a delay help? One explanation: early on the writer cannot yet look up legal roads by itself, so it leans on the hint, and the hint is a blurry average. It follows the blur and writes illegal moves. Later the writer is strong enough to ignore blur. This explanation fits five earlier observations, but it has never been tested directly. Treat it as a guess.

The first gate: is a model good enough to be a teacher?

S2 will learn from an S1 model, so that model has to be competent. Gate G1 sets the bar on the development puzzles: at least 80% on standard and at least 95% on lookup. Seed 901 of A_delayed scored 19.3% and 27.2%, so it failed. Seed 902 scored 84.2% and 100%, so it passed. As planned, S2 uses seed 902 as one teacher and V8's older seed 811 as the other.

Several sharp guesses instead of one blur

When a step has several valid next moves, can the model keep a few distinct, committed guesses instead of averaging them into mush, and can it learn which one to try?

In one breath

  • S1's guess of the next concept is an average. When two moves are equally good, the average points at neither.
  • S2 gives the model K "what if" codes. Each code commits to one possible next concept, and a selector learns the odds that each one is right.
  • We compare K = 8 against K = 1 on two seeds. The first half (dense core) is running now. The second half (recurrent core) is queued after S6's first experiment.

The problem: the average of two good answers can be bad

Go back to the fork from the basics chapter. A friend asks which way to turn. Half your sources say left and half say right. If you answer with the average, "walk straight ahead", you walk into the wall.

S1's guess of the next concept is trained with squared distance. Mathematically, the best single guess under squared distance is the average of everything that could happen. On GraphNav the true next concept depends on which colour the walk takes, and often several colours are equally fine. The average of those concepts is the concept of no real road.

Watch it happen. Three real options are marked. One guess is trained by gradient descent on examples from them.

Analogy

A group of art students is asked to paint "a face". If each student is graded on how close their painting is to every reference photo, all of them paint the same blurry average face. If each reference photo is assigned only to the closest student, the students specialise and paint distinct, sharp faces.

Step 1: give the last concept block K codes

We do not build a new network. We reuse the last concept block and run it K times, each time with a different learnable code added to its input. A code is just a short vector of numbers, like a dial setting. Each setting nudges the block toward a different answer.

This is cheap. The block's keys and values come from the plain input, so one pass over the sequence serves all K codes. At K = 8 the extra cost is about 29% more block-work, and only 3,208 new parameters.

Safety net

With K = 1 there are no codes at all, and with the closed loop off the training is exactly V8's. A test checks that the loss, every gradient and one optimiser step match V8 to one part in a million. That is how S2 is "nested" in V8: K = 1 is the old method, so any difference is caused by K.

Step 2: winner-take-all training

For each position we compare every hypothesis with the true next concept. The closest hypothesis gets a full lesson. The others get a lesson of only 5% strength. The 5% keeps losers from being abandoned, so they can still move toward a mode later. Without it, a code that loses early may never win again.

Step 3: a selector learns the odds

Having K guesses is no use if you cannot tell which to try. A small linear layer, the selector, reads the plain row and predicts how likely each code is to be the winner. Its training target is a soft version of "who won": codes that nearly tied share the credit, so near-ties do not become noise.

Now the writer, the frozen decoder, needs a plan to follow during training. Which code's guess should we hand it? There are three options, and the choice matters.

The main arm uses m = 2: draw two codes using the selector's odds and let the closer one steer the writer. It is a middle road. It sends the lesson to a code that already explains the truth well, as winner-take-all does, but it still only offers codes the selector would actually offer at test time.

Step 4: a frozen teacher and a trainable student

S2 does not retrain the whole model. It takes a finished S1 model, the teacher, and freezes the encoder, the decoder, the codebook and the sticker heads. Only a fresh copy of the concept stack, the new codes and the selector are trainable. Look at which parts are grey (frozen) in the 3D scene below.

The teacher also supplies a safety rope: for the first 10% of updates the plain no-code path is pulled hard toward the teacher's outputs, and the pull fades to zero by 30%. This keeps the model from drifting before the codes have settled.

Which teacher?

Seed 911 learns from S1's seed-902 model. Seed 912 learns from V8's seed-811 model, because S1's other seed failed the competence gate. So the two S2 seeds really are different teachers, and arms should be compared within a seed.

Step 5: practise on your own handwriting

When search runs, the concept stack will read its own guesses instead of real concepts. If it has only ever read real ones, that is like a driver who has only practised on a simulator and is then put on a real road. So, in 25% of training sequences, a short run of 1 to 4 chunks gets its input replaced by the model's own committed guess. The grading target stays the real concept.

Step 6: when a hypothesis never wins

Winner-take-all training has a known failure: a code that never wins never learns, so it is wasted. The project handles this with the 5% loser lesson, a restart rule, and logging. The rule: every 500 updates, any code whose share of wins fell below one in ten K is restarted as a noisy copy of the most-winning code.

How we decide: four gates in plain words

All thresholds were written down before the first S2 run. Each gate asks one question. They are judged on the development puzzles, never the sealed ones, with 95% bootstrap intervals. Each gate is judged per seed, and passing needs both seeds to pass.

G2.1

Authority: does the code steer the writer?

(a) If we pick the best code using the answer, is the loss on the puzzle tokens at least 0.05 nats lower than K = 1? (b) If we swap the chosen code for each other code at the last prompt chunk, does the first road change in at least 30% of cases, and is the new road legal in at least 90% of those?

pass: ≥ 0.05 nats and ≥ 30% / ≥ 90% · fail: no gain, or < 15% / < 70%
G2.2

Calibration: can the odds be used without peeking?

Four checks: the loss with one drawn code and with the top code stays close to K = 1; the selector's top two choices cover the right next move almost as often as the best possible selector could; its stated odds match how often codes win; and greedy puzzle success is not worse than K = 1 by more than 2 points.

pass: ≤ +0.05 / +0.02 nats, coverage ≥ 90% of the ceiling, error ≤ 0.05, ≥ K/2 live codes, success within 2 points
G2.3

Imagined versus executed

Take N steps by following codes in the model's head, then take the same steps by actually writing each one. Do the two end up making the same next decision, and are the written steps legal?

pass at every N up to H: agreement ≥ 90%, legal ≥ 95%. The largest passing H is the lookahead depth S5 may use.
G2.4

Execution consistency: what does each code write?

For a given state, decode every code. Do they write at least two distinct legal roads when two roads make progress? Is there always some code that makes progress? Is a code's guess closest to the road it actually writes?

pass: branching ≥ 80% and coverage ≥ 95%. The guess-matches-road check (≥ 90%) is needed only for the imagine-everything variant.
What a failure would mean
  • G2.1 fails on both seeds: the code does not steer the writer. The concept branches of S4 and S5 stop, and the project reports evidence against concept-level control at this scale.
  • G2.2 fails but G2.1 passes: codes work if you look at the answer, but the selector cannot be trusted alone. Search can still use it as a prior, but plain greedy results carry no claim.
  • G2.4 fails: the codes write the same thing. Decoder-free search is dropped, and only the grounded control remains.
  • G2.3 fails at depth H: search may look only as deep as the passing depth.

Letting the writer adapt without breaking anything

After S2 the writer has only ever seen blurry hints, but it will now be handed sharp, committed ones. Can we retrain gently so it gets used to them without erasing what S2 built?

In one breath

  • The decoder (the writer) learned from averaged hints in S1 and was frozen in S2. Now it is handed committed guesses it never practised on.
  • S3 tries three ways of continuing training, from the most cautious (only the writer learns) to the boldest (everything learns).
  • An arm replaces the S2 model only if it passes a gate against it. If none passes, S2 goes forward unchanged and S3 is reported as a negative result.

The mismatch S3 is about

Think of a translator who learned a language from textbooks (blurry, averaged examples) and is now handed real, sharp phrases. They probably cope, but a little practice on the real thing would help. That is the hope of S3, and the project's original idea calls it "continue training until the refitted module is stable".

There is also a danger. In another research project (called ETA), training a base model together with a concept module made the concept module collapse, while keeping the base frozen did not. That is a reason to try the cautious version first and to keep a frozen control. It is not proof that careful joint training must fail, and S3 is built to find out.

Not run yet

S3 starts only after S2's dense tier finishes and a model is promoted. Nothing on this page about S3 is a result. It is the plan and its reasons.

Why the order of the three arms matters

The concept core reads concepts and predicts concepts, and those concepts are made by the encoder. If you train the encoder, every concept moves, even if the core itself is frozen. The core then reads a map that has shifted under its feet.

For that reason the cautious arm changes the decoder only. The concept coordinates stay put. A separate, optional arm changes the encoder too, and measures how far the concepts drift.

A check before any training: the transplant test

Before S3 trains anything, it asks how well the S2 student can stand in for the original teacher. The decoder is given a mixture of the two plans, from all teacher to all student, and we watch how the results change. With all teacher (λ = 0) the output must match the teacher exactly. A test enforces it.

Learning from a right-or-wrong checker

Can the model get better at choosing among its guesses by playing puzzles and learning from which choices the checker approves?

In one breath

  • Post-training means learning from feedback after the main training. Here the only feedback is the verifier saying right or wrong.
  • The main method is expert iteration: search finds good choices with the verifier's help, and then the quick instincts (the selector and two tiny judges) are trained to reproduce them. Repeat four times.
  • It is compared, with the same puzzles and the same budget, against three standard methods. The result is reported either way.

Why there is no separate fine-tuning step

Many pipelines first fine-tune on worked examples and then use reinforcement learning. Here, the training data already contains verified worked examples: the puzzle answers are trained response-only from the very start. A separate fine-tuning stage would only repeat that. What is new in S4 is learning from a checker on puzzles the examples do not teach well, mostly the hard ones.

The primary method: expert iteration

Imagine a chess player who spends a long time analysing a position, makes the move that analysis points to, and keeps notes. After many games they train their instinct to suggest what the analysis would have concluded, so next time the instinct is better and the analysis starts from a better place. That is expert iteration. The famous AlphaZero uses the same idea.

What each trained part learns to predict

After a search, we know how often each code was visited. A code that looked promising was tried many times. We train the selector so that its odds match those visit shares. In plain terms, we copy the slow search into the quick instinct.

The two tiny judges are single linear layers (129 numbers each). The value judge predicts whether the answer will be correct, from the current position. The stop judge predicts whether stopping is legal here. They let the search score a branch without paying for a full rollout. They are only trusted if they pass their own gate, checked on held-out positions: the value judge needs an AUROC of at least 0.80 on complete answers and 0.70 on intermediate and imagined ones, and the stop judge needs 90% recall with at most 2% false positives on imagined states. If a judge fails, search falls back to verified completions, which cost more.

Is it a fair fight?

Three standard methods start from the same promoted checkpoint, with the same puzzles, the same number of verifier calls and the same time cap. Token RFT samples complete answers, keeps the verified ones and fine-tunes on them. Token GRPO is a popular policy-gradient method on the same adapter. Selector REINFORCE is policy gradient on the selector. The first two use a small trainable adapter (LoRA) on the decoder instead of the codes.

The capacity is not equal, and the project says so openly.

GradCuit and the sign of the reward

GradCuit is a test-time method. When an answer fails, it nudges one code vector, up to ten times, to make the rejected answer less likely, and then writes again. One detail is easy to get wrong. If the reward were simply 1 for correct and 0 for wrong, a wrong answer would multiply the whole gradient by zero and the method would do nothing at all. So the reward is set to o − 1: 0 when accepted, −1 when rejected.

Planning ahead without writing words

If checking a concept costs a fraction of writing words, can the model plan several steps ahead by searching over concepts, and does that beat the best ordinary search at the same cost?

In one breath

  • A search tries many possible futures and keeps looking at the promising ones. Writing out words for every future is expensive.
  • S5 searches over concepts instead. The decoder only writes words for the one step the model commits to, and then the search starts again from the new real position.
  • The idea is compared with a search over tokens and with simply sampling many answers, under the same time and checker budget. The design's own confidence that the new idea wins is about 25%.

What a search is, in plain words

Suppose you must pick one of four doors, and you can peek behind each one but peeking costs time. A sensible plan is to peek at the most promising door, see what is there, and then decide whether to peek again at the same door or try another. Tree search, specifically Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), does exactly that, thousands of times, and then chooses the door it peeked at most.

Each peek (a simulation) does four things: select a branch to try, expand it, evaluate how good it looks, and back that result up into the average for the branch. The branch is chosen by a score that adds two things: how well the branch has done so far (Q), and an exploration bonus that is larger for branches the model's prior likes (P) and branches tried rarely (small visit count N). This rule is called PUCT.

Show the formula
score(a) = Q(a) + c * P(a) * sqrt(N_total) / (1 + N(a))        c = 1.5
Q(a) = average result of the tries of branch a
P(a) = the selector's prior for code a (and the STOP judge for STOP)
N(a) = how many times branch a has been tried

At the very first simulation every score is zero, and ties go to the lowest-numbered action. With a tiny budget this makes the search pick code 1 by rule. The project notes it as a property of the formula, not a malfunction.

Why concepts make a search cheaper

In a token tree, every branch must be written out: four tokens through the encoder and the decoder, one after another. That is about 28 units of work per try. In a concept tree a try is just one code plus one step of the concept stack: about 5 units. The decoder never runs inside the lookahead. This is the "decoder removed from the search" part of the original proposal. The decoder still runs once to commit to each real step.

Plan, commit one step, replan

A GPS does not plan the whole trip once and then go blind. It plans a few turns ahead, you drive one stretch, and it plans again from where you really are. S5's main method works the same way and is called receding horizon, written RH(H) where H is how many steps it imagines ahead.

  1. Search from a real position.

    The root is the prompt plus every step already written and re-read.

  2. Imagine up to H steps, with no decoder.

    Each try follows a code, takes one cheap step in concept space, and asks the judges how good it looks.

  3. Commit to the most-visited code.

    Write its four tokens with the decoder and read them back in.

  4. Search again from the new real position.

    Stop when the best action is STOP, and send the answer to the checker.

RH(1) imagines just one step, and it is where S5 begins. There are two other shapes, both treated as experiments on the side. G(R) runs a single search for the whole puzzle and re-grounds, meaning writes and re-reads, every R steps. IMAG imagines the entire path and decodes only the winner at the very end.

How far ahead can we trust the imagination?

An imagined step is never perfect, and errors add up. If each imagined step is 95% right, then twenty in a row are right only about 36% of the time. So the lookahead depth has to be limited, and the limit is not guessed. Gate G2.3 from S2 measures it, and S5 may look ahead only as deep as the gate allows. That is why receding horizon re-reads the real position after every committed step.

The direct answer to the original question

Can the decoder be removed while searching over concepts? On this exam, in principle yes: one concept is exactly one hop, the rule for the next step is a lookup over roads the concept stack already attends to, and in an earlier version (V5), training on NCP made a model's own fed-back guesses about 18 times less harmful on graph data. What could stop it is a list, and each item has its own gate:

  • Codes that do not write distinct legal roads (G2.4).
  • Imagined and executed positions that lead to different decisions (G2.3).
  • Judges trained on real positions that do not transfer to imagined ones (G4).
  • Wall-clock time dominated by launch overhead instead of arithmetic. This is measured first, before any comparison.

The answer will be a table over H and R, not a yes or no.

Learning new skills without forgetting the old ones

Can the model learn new tasks one after another by adding small adapters that switch on only for the inputs they were made for, and does that protect what it already knew?

In one breath

  • When a network learns something new, it tends to overwrite what it knew. This is called catastrophic forgetting.
  • A method called LSL (Local Support Learning) gives each new task its own small adapter plus a gate that opens the adapter only for inputs that look like that task.
  • S6 adds three tasks in a row and compares six ways of learning. It also tests whether the gate helps at all, which an earlier try (V8) never did.

The problem: new learning overwrites old learning

Learn Spanish intensely for a month and your French gets rusty. Networks do something worse. They store everything in one shared set of numbers, so training on a new task moves numbers that an old task needed. The old skill can vanish almost completely.

This is not a hypothetical for this project. V8 tried a version of LSL and got a mixed picture.

What this does and does not show
  • It is evidence against that tiny setup on that sequence of tasks.
  • It is not a measurement of what gating is worth, because the control that leaves the adapter always on was never run.
  • It does not refute the LSL method. The paper's smallest model was about 600 times bigger than ours, and the paper says retention improves with scale.
  • It does not confirm why phase 2 failed. Bind shares its road-list format with navigation, which makes overlap likely, but nobody measured it on old-task inputs.

Step 1: a thin add-on instead of rewriting the weights

Instead of changing a big weight matrix, we leave it frozen and add a small correction to it. The correction is built from two thin matrices, B and A, multiplied together. The thinner they are, the fewer numbers there are to learn. The thickness is called the rank r. This trick is called LoRA.

Step 2: a gate that opens only for familiar inputs

An adapter that is always on still changes the answer for every input, including old tasks. LSL adds a gate in front of each adapter. The gate asks one question: does this input look like the data this adapter was trained on, or like everything else? It answers using two density models, one fitted to the new task's inputs and one fitted to generic inputs, and it opens the adapter only when the first is higher. A smoothing rule then stops the gate from flickering from one token to the next.

The theory in one line

Nothing in the training of the new task controls where the gate opens outside that task. And wherever the supports overlap, the newest update wins by design. So whether LSL protects anything depends on whether old and new inputs can be told apart at each matrix. S6 measures that separability first, then judges LSL.

Three tasks in a row

A stated limit

Choosing the second task and checking the gates uses inputs from old tasks, taken from a separate calibration set. So the claim is "no replay of old data while the adapter learns", not "no access to old data at all".

The queue, and how results become decisions

What has run, what is running, what is waiting, and the rules that turn a number into a decision.

The queue

All experiments share one laptop GPU, so they run one after another. The order was chosen by the project owner: finish S1, then the dense half of S2, then S6's first experiment, then the recurrent half of S2, then S3, S4, S5 and the final analysis. S2 and S6 do not run at the same time, because sharing the GPU would save no time and would spoil the speed measurements.

How a result becomes a decision

A number from a run is never the decision. Three habits stand between a number and a conclusion.

  1. Zones, not points.

    Every gate has a pass zone, a partial band and a fail zone, and the whole 95% interval must sit in a zone for it to count. The slider figure below shows how it works.

  2. Paired comparisons.

    Two arms are compared on the same puzzles, one by one. For each puzzle we ask whether arm A solved it and B did not, or the reverse. In S1, on seed 902, delayed beat no-NCP on 121 puzzles and lost on 101: a gap that is too small to rule out luck.

  3. At least two seeds.

    A gate passes only if both seeds pass. One pass and one fail is "seed-dependent", and counts as inconclusive. S1 showed how much one seed can mislead.
