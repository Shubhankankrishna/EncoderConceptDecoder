The V9 experiments, from zero

Six experiments, S1 to S6, that all ask one question: can a small language model think in whole steps instead of single words, and can we search over those steps? This page assumes you have never trained a neural network.

The big idea in sixty seconds

A language model writes text one small piece at a time. V9 asks what happens if it also keeps a short summary note for every four pieces and learns to guess the next note before it writes the words.

Here is the whole project in plain words. A normal language model reads a piece of text and guesses the next small piece, called a token. It does this over and over, and that is how it writes.

V9 adds one more habit. After every four tokens the model writes a short summary note and keeps it. We call that note a concept. The model is also trained to guess the next concept before it has written the words that belong to it. That is a bit like glancing at the next chapter title before you read the chapter.

If this works, two useful things become possible:

  • Cheap what-if thinking. Looking at a summary note costs far less than writing out the words. A model could try several different next ideas and only write words for the best one.
  • Planning ahead. With cheap what-ifs, the model can plan a few steps ahead the way a chess engine does, and then write.

Whether any of this really works is the open question. The six experiments below are the way we find out, one claim at a time.

Why six experiments and not one big one?

The earlier versions of this project (V1 to V8) kept changing many things at once. When a result came out good or bad, nobody could say which change caused it. V9 follows a simple rule: each experiment changes one thing and measures it against a control. A control is the same setup with that one thing switched off.

Idea

Think of a taste test. To learn whether adding salt helps a soup, you cook two pots that differ only in salt. If you also change the onions, the stock and the pot, you learn nothing about salt.

Every experiment also writes down, before it runs, what would count as success. This is called pre-registration. The pass or fail rules are called gates, and you will meet them in each chapter.

How to read this page

  • Read it top to bottom. Each chapter starts from zero and only uses ideas explained earlier.
  • Every framed figure with buttons is interactive. Slide, click and press play.
  • The 3D scenes rotate when you drag them. Scroll or pinch to zoom, and click a part to read what it does.
  • Boxes marked Pause and think are good places to stop for a second.
  • Words with a dotted underline have a hover definition. All of them are collected in the glossary at the end.
Read this about the numbers

Only the S1 results on this page are final, sealed test results. The S2 numbers are development diagnostics: checks made while the experiment is still running, which decide nothing. S3 to S6 have not been run, so those chapters describe the design and the questions, never results.
